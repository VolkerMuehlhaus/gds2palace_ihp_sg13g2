# Choosing a mesh for your Palace simulation

If you're not sure how fine to mesh your gds2palace model, or whether adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) is worth turning on, this page summarizes what we found across six mesh convergence studies on real IHP SG13G2 (and one SG13CMOS5L) structures: a spiral inductor, a transformer, a D-band coupled-line balun, a 2:1 edge-coupled balun, a MIM-capacitor-loaded balun, and a MOM capacitor. Each study reruns the same layout at several mesh settings and compares the results, so you can see directly what accuracy you get for what runtime/memory cost.

## Quick recommendations

- **Start with a mesh cell size that gives you at least 2-3 cells across your narrowest trace or gap.** The structures here have trace widths and gaps from 2-8 µm, and a cell size resolving that gap with 2-3 cells consistently gave results within a percent or two of the finest mesh tried, at a fraction of the runtime. Going coarser than the gap itself is the worst offender — one study's 5 µm mesh, wider than its own 2 µm coupled-line gap, was clearly the least accurate point in the entire set (and a different study's coarsest point crashed the solver outright).
- **Use order 2 (Palace's default) for any result you plan to report or trust.** Order 1 runs several times faster, but gave up accuracy in every study — in one case a fairly constant offset regardless of mesh refinement, in another a frequency/depth shift that shrank (but didn't fully close) as the mesh refined. Either way, order 1 is fine for a quick rough look (e.g. checking your port setup or layout), not for final numbers — and don't assume a finer order-1 mesh reliably closes the gap to order 2. The [MOM capacitor study](mesh_convergence_mom_cmos5l/mesh_convergence_report.md) found the same pattern one order up: order 2 vs. order 3 left a ~1.2% gap in capacitance even at the finest mesh tried, so for a weakly-coupled capacitive structure, order 2 alone may not be enough either — worth checking order 3 if the exact value matters.
- **Don't assume AMR will "just work" if you give it enough iterations.** In every study that let it run more than 2 iterations, AMR kept refining well past the point where the answer had already stopped changing, burning much more time and memory for no real accuracy gain — and even after using its entire iteration budget, it still hadn't satisfied its own convergence target. Capped at 2 iterations, results were mixed: sometimes it beat a directly-run uniform mesh of comparable accuracy, sometimes a plain uniform mesh was just as good for less cost. Either way, cap it around 2 iterations and compare against a uniform mesh before trusting it blindly.
- **When in doubt, run two mesh sizes and compare.** That's really all a convergence study is — if your result doesn't change much between a medium and a fine mesh, you're probably fine; if it does, go finer.

## The six studies

- **[Spiral inductor](mesh_convergence_inductor/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a simple 2-port coil. Inductance and Q factor were evaluated directly, alongside S-parameters. This is the easiest case: even a fairly coarse mesh gets inductance right to within about 1%, and it's a study that also compares FEM order 1 vs. order 2.  
Note this is a single-ended inductor, where current in neighboring turns flows in a similar direction. This means that the local field between adjacent traces changes less sharply than in a differential (symmetric) inductor, where neighboring traces of the two half-coils carry opposite-phase current. That likely makes this structure gentler on the mesh than a differential inductor of similar dimensions would be.
- **[Transformer](mesh_convergence_transformer/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a 5-port coupled-coil transformer with a center tap, targeting 30 GHz. Covers mixed-mode S-parameters of the bare coil pair. This test structure does **not** include the MIM capacitors the real matching network uses to compensate the transformer's imaginary part, so no real-load/floating-impedance result is reported — a bare-coil number there would misrepresent the actual circuit (see the report's §7 for why).
- **[D-band balun](mesh_convergence_D-band_balun/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a compact edge-coupled-line balun for 140-170 GHz. The smallest, most tightly-coupled geometry of the five, and the one where mesh choice mattered most.
- **[2:1 edge-coupled balun](mesh_convergence_balun2x1/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a single octagonal loop of 4 edge-coupled lines (8 µm width, 2 µm gap) giving a 2:1 primary:secondary turns ratio, no center tap. Also compares order 1 vs. order 2; unlike the inductor study, the order-1 error here shrinks as the mesh refines rather than staying roughly constant — a reminder that this trade-off is structure-dependent. Same caveat as the transformer: no MIM caps in this test structure, so no real-load impedance result is reported, only the mixed-mode S-parameters at the true 200 Ω/50 Ω system impedances.
- **[MIM-loaded balun](mesh_convergence_balun_mim/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a 3-port balun (1 single-ended port at a true 80 Ω, plus a differential pair at a true 100 Ω) for 17-22 GHz, this time **with** its MIM capacitors included in the model. Covers the mixed single-ended/differential-mode S-parameters at those true impedances, plus amplitude/phase balance between the two differential outputs (both within a fraction of a dB/degree of ideal here). Also includes a separate 1-port check of one MIM capacitor's simulated capacitance against its nominal (GDS-text-label) value — mesh refinement closed part of the gap, but a real parasitic contribution from the port-to-cap routing remained (see that report's §5 for the breakdown).
- **[MOM capacitor](mesh_convergence_mom_cmos5l/mesh_convergence_report.md)** — a 2-port interdigitated MOM cap on the new **SG13CMOS5L** stackup, evaluated at a single fixed frequency (1 GHz, no sweep) rather than a band. Tracks C12 (port1-port2 mutual capacitance, from the raw Y-parameters) and delta-S convergence across 3 mesh sizes, and is the only study here that crosses mesh refinement with a full FEM order comparison (order 2 vs. **order 3**, not order 1 vs. 2): order 3 converges faster and settles ~1.2% below order 2's finest-mesh answer, a gap mesh refinement alone doesn't fully close. Also shows C12 vs. frequency (0-50 GHz) at the coarsest setting, for reference.

## Bottom line

These are six different structures at six different frequency ranges, so the exact numbers won't transfer directly to your design — but the pattern was consistent across most of them: a moderately fine uniform mesh (order 2) got close to the best achievable answer quickly and predictably, while chasing AMR's default convergence target or dropping to order 1 for speed both came with real trade-offs, and the size of those trade-offs varied by structure. The MOM capacitor study is the one exception worth flagging: there, order 2 stayed ~1.2% off from order 3's finest-mesh answer even at the finest mesh tried, so "order 2 is enough" isn't a universal rule — if your structure is a weakly-coupled capacitive one, it's worth checking order 3 rather than assuming mesh refinement alone will close the gap. If your structure is broadly similar in scale to one of these six, start from that study's recommended mesh settings and verify with one finer run (and, for capacitive structures, one higher-order run) before trusting the result.

## A note on Palace's own error indicator

These reports don't show Palace's internal `error-indicators.csv` Norm/Max values. That indicator is a relative, energy-normalized FEM residual — a genuinely different kind of number from the mesh-quality or Delta-S convergence criteria you may know from other solvers (HFSS, CST) — and Palace's own documentation notes it "is not a strict relative-error bound for every derived quantity such as S-parameters." Reporting it alongside S-parameter/capacitance results caused more confusion than it resolved, so every study on this page judges convergence directly from `Max|ΔS|` (and, where relevant, the target quantity itself — inductance, capacitance, etc.) computed from the actual simulated outputs instead.
